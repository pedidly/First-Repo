# Outreach

The listings bring in €12 orders. This file brings in the €300 ones.

---

## The walk-in

Five businesses, one afternoon, in September. Bike shop, hobby store, café,
garden centre, small manufacturer or workshop. Bring three printed samples in a
box — a name sign, a repaired part, a branded table number.

> "Hi — I make small plastic parts locally. Do you ever need something you
> can't buy any more, or something that doesn't exist?"

Then stop talking. The silence does the work.

**If they say no:** "No problem. If one turns up, here's my card." Leave. That
is a complete, successful visit — you were remembered at zero cost.

**If they hesitate:** "Things like a replacement knob, a jig to hold something
while you work on it, a bracket that broke." Concrete examples unlock the ones
who have a problem but don't think of it as a 3D printing problem.

**If they say yes:** photo of the part, next to a ruler, before you leave. Quote
within 24 hours. Never quote in the room — you will guess low.

---

## The follow-up email

> **Subject:** The part we talked about
>
> Hi [name],
>
> Good to meet you on [day]. I've had a look at the [part] — I can make it, and
> it comes to €[X] for the first one, €[Y] each after that if you want spares.
>
> Turnaround is [3] days from go-ahead. If you'd like one, just reply and I'll
> start it.
>
> [Your name]
> [phone]

Short. No pitch, no explanation of the technology, no attachments. One number
and one question.

## The market application

Most Christmas and craft markets close applications six to eight weeks out.
Apply in September for anything in November or December.

> **What I sell:** Personalised plastic homeware and gifts, made locally — name
> signs, keychains, ornaments and pet tags, each one made to order with the
> customer's own name on it.
>
> **Price range:** €10 to €30.
>
> **Stall:** One 1.8 m table. I bring my own lighting and a card reader.
>
> **What makes it work at a market:** I personalise on the spot — customers pick
> a name and a colour and collect it before they leave. The machine runs on the
> stall, which stops people as they walk past.
>
> **Power:** [one 230 V socket, 150 W] — or say "none required" if you batch
> everything in advance.

Apply to two. Expect one to say no.

---

## Quoting a repair job

Reply within 24 hours, always, even if the answer is no. Speed is most of why
they picked you.

> Thanks for the photos. I can make this.
>
> It's €[X], which includes modelling it from your photos and one revision if
> the first one doesn't fit. It'll be ready by [date].
>
> One thing to flag: this part [takes load / gets warm / holds a hinge], so I'd
> print it in PETG rather than standard PLA. Same price.
>
> Say the word and I'll start.

**If it's out of scope:**

> I've had a look and I'd rather not take this one — [it carries real load / it
> sits against something hot], and a printed part would eventually fail there.
> You want [a metal part from a bearing supplier / the OEM part, still listed as
> [number]]. No charge, obviously. If something else breaks, send it over.

Turning down the wrong job is how you get sent the right one.

---

## When they say it's expensive

Do not discount. Explain, once:

> It's four hours of machine time plus the modelling — I'm making one of these,
> not pulling it off a shelf. A new [appliance] is €[300]. If it's more than
> you want to spend, no hard feelings.

Then stop. If they walk, they were never going to be a good customer. Every
discount you give is repeated to the next four people who ask.

---

## The card

Cheap, and it does the whole job:

> **[Your name]** — 3D printing, [town]
> Broken part? Send me a photo.
> [phone] · [email]

Every market buyer leaves with one. December gift buyers become January repair
customers, and repair customers are the only ones who come back every month.
